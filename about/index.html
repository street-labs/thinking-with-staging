How it started

We started Thinking With… in 2020, during the depths of the pandemic. We were all stuck, largely isolated, and intellectually starving — teaching online, our usual conferences canceled, with little of the ordinary opportunity to think and talk with other people. Then, on some random day in June, John called Nathaniel and said, “hey, you should read this really smart dissertation proposal I just got from a student of mine, Nate DeProspo.” Things spiraled from there. Nathaniel and Nate met, along with John, on this new thing called “Zoom,” to talk about Nate's project — and to ask him why in the world he was so invested in G. W. F. Hegel of all people.

We kept Zooming, as a reading group. After a while we imagined our discussions were for an audience. It was something of a joke at first — us, starting a podcast on esoteric philosophy. But it got us thinking about how we'd relate to listeners. If we were to make a podcast, what would the goal be, especially since we spend most of our time teaching in a classroom? There's no shortage of material on the internet aimed at teaching. And one of the ideas we kept circling back to is that the most powerful teaching and learning we do has little to do with presenting and remembering propositions, arguments, and histories. For us, philosophy and rhetoric are about teaching styles of thinking — and we learn styles of thinking by getting swept up in a current.

We wanted our podcast to be a stream to get caught up in, for our listeners and for us. We wanted to “think with.” The question was, think with what? The answer had to be ambiguous and precise at once: think with each other, with texts, with intellectual traditions and longstanding but ever-mutating questions and concepts. We wanted to imagine that our listeners weren't taking notes for an exam but were swept up and thinking along with us — and imagining that audience shaped how we read, spoke, and positioned ourselves while recording. That's why we added the ellipsis: the whole idea was an experiment in thinking with… an ever-changing complex of things.

Why Hegel, and what came after

We recorded our first “season” on Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit. If the goal had been a big audience, this may have been the single worst text we could have chosen: early-19th-century, infamously dense, and — despite Hegel's enormous influence — the subject of a critical mass of critiques across the 20th century. Who reads Hegel anymore? What rhetorical theorist reads Hegel? Almost no one. Except Nate DeProspo, who thought Hegel shows us something about the nature of subjectivity that most contemporary theories would rather wish away. So we read the Phenomenology to better understand Hegel, to better understand Nate's affinity for him, and to launch the maiden voyage of the show.

The season was a pedagogical experiment. We read the book slowly. Very slowly — a few pages per episode. We read passages closely, tried to understand them, and thought with them. We published twelve episodes and covered the preface: a whole forty-five pages. We liked it so much we did it again — with Gilles Deleuze's The Logic of Sense; and again with Martin Heidegger's What Is Called Thinking?; and again with a set of essays and chapters by Jacques Derrida; and again with Friedrich Nietzsche's The Gay Science; and again with Heidegger's Being and Time.

What's changing

After six years, we want to change things up a little. The main goal is the same: this is still a pedagogical experiment in provoking styles of thinking by thinking with… But we're organizing things a bit differently. First, there's this website — with archives of our seasons, this page so you can actually know who we are, and ways to contact us and subscribe.

We're also reorganizing our upcoming season. We're making a concerted effort to briefly overview the focus of each episode to orient listeners before we dive in. And we're organizing the season around an idea — “Critique” — rather than a single text. Alongside the episodes, we'll post our thoughts and responses to the texts we read, to engage and provoke our listeners on the questions we're working through, and we'll publish something of a “syllabus” for our schedule of readings so you can keep track of where we are and where we're going. We're also planning to co-write a more robust essay, likely for an academic journal, and to dedicate a few episodes to a real-time glimpse of what it's like to write and publish an academic article.

So stay tuned. Things are going to be different. But also the same. Is there any other option?
